What the assault-victim gap measures

2015 to 2025 · three sources · Martin Ngoh

The 59-city pageCodemartinngoh.com

Police record assaults on Black women about 4.2 times as often as on White women across large US cities, and hospital emergency departments see a gap of the same size (4.6 times for women). Only the national victimization survey, in which women describe their own assaults, puts it near 1. The police gap is not an artifact of policing: in 3 of 4 cities police write up the same share of victims per 911 assault call whatever a neighborhood's racial makeup.

The question
Is the 4x gap in police-recorded assaults on Black women a fact about assaults, about reporting, or about how police record?
  • Reporting is not it: Black women report 54% of their assaults to police, White women 47%, which lifts the survey's ratio only to about 1.4 in large places.
  • Recording is not it: victims per 911 assault call are flat across neighborhoods in New York, Los Angeles, New Orleans, and rise only a little in Baltimore (1.2 times at most).
  • Repeat counting is not it: in Los Angeles, Black and White women both average about 1.1 police reports each; counting women instead of reports moves the ratio from 5.7 to 5.65.
  • Hospitals agree with police: emergency department visits for assault run 4.6 times higher for Black women than White women (interval 3.25 to 6.61), with no call to police involved.
  • What remains open is why the survey sees so little of it. Each Black woman who answers stands in for a quarter more women than each White woman who answers, and the survey does not reach shelters or institutions.

What police write up per call

Flat lines mean police record the same share of victims whatever the neighborhood. New York: +0.06 (SE 0.065), Los Angeles: +0.08 (SE 0.107), New Orleans: -0.00 (SE 0.119), Baltimore: +0.20 (SE 0.07).

Where the calls come from

Assault calls per resident rise with a neighborhood's Black share: New York 2.77x, Los Angeles 2.1x, New Orleans 1.78x, Baltimore 2.32x. The gap is already there when the phone rings.

Method

Survey: BJS NCVS Select files, women 18 and older, aggravated and simple assault, series-adjusted weights, 2015 to 2024. Hospitals: NHAMCS 2021 and 2022 emergency department files. Police: the 59-city run's victim files and ACS populations. Calls: each city's open data, grouped by neighborhood, residents from ACS tracts assigned by centroid. Generated by scripts/build_page.py from out/*.json; the README holds every table.

Limits
  • Small survey cells. 919 Black women assault victims in the survey over ten years; the hospital figure rests on 60 sampled visits. Intervals are approximate.
  • Definitions differ. Survey simple assault includes threats; police and hospital counts do not. The injury-only checks address this.
  • Place is not residence. Assaults are recorded where they happen and residents counted where they live, so the within-neighborhood ratios are rough too.
  • Race recorded three ways. By officers in police data, by respondents in the survey, by hospital staff in NHAMCS. The race-coding bound from the national run applies to the police side.
  • What is left open. Why the survey sees so little: non-response, coverage of shelters and institutions, or what women say at home. And who makes the calls. Neither can be settled with public data.
  • What, not why. Nothing here measures causes, offenders or circumstances.
